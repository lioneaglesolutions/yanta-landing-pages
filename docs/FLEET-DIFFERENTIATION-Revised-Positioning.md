# Revised positioning — you buried the lead
### The 14T with Trimble Earthworks and rubber pads, and the 7T telehandler, change the whole strategy

---

## What you just told me, and why it matters

I built the original plan around "15T and 35T excavators, telehandler, utes and vans" — a generic fleet with no obvious edge, competing against eight suppliers inside 30km of Rocky. On that basis, rural acreage work was the right target: reachable on Facebook, low compliance, fast decisions.

**That was the wrong read, because the fleet isn't generic.** Three specs change it:

| Spec | What it actually means |
|---|---|
| **Trimble Earthworks on the 14T** | Semi-automatic 3D machine control. Dig to design, no spotter on the ground, no string lines, less rework, better material yield. Contractors using it report roughly **halving** their time on grade work. |
| **Rubber pads on the tracks** | The 14T can track across concrete, asphalt, pavers and finished surfaces **without wrecking them** — no plywood, no track mats, no resurfacing bill. |
| **7 tonne telehandler** | Most hire fleets stock 2.5–4T. A 7T is a genuinely scarce machine, and with a jib fitted it legally requires a **CN non-slewing mobile crane licence** to operate. |

**The strategic consequence: flip the priority.** Contractors and commercial work should be primary; rural acreage becomes the filler, not the lead.

Here's why. A bloke wanting his dam cleaned out does not care about Trimble Earthworks or rubber pads — he buys on price, and you'd be racing seven competitors to the bottom. A civil contractor who's just won a subdivision and doesn't own machine control **cares enormously**, will pay a premium, and can't easily get it anywhere else in Rockhampton.

**Scarce capability is far easier to sell than commodity capacity.** You have scarce capability and you weren't leading with it.

---

## Proof that the capability alone is a market

There are businesses in Australia whose entire model is renting Trimble cab kits to contractors who don't own machine control — SITECH Solutions, Machine Control Solutions and others. Their pitch is "run GPS on your GPS-ready excavator without the capital outlay", and they claim the kit often pays for itself on the first project.

**Read that again:** contractors pay real money to rent *just the technology*, then supply their own machine and operator.

You can rent them the technology **and** the machine **and** the operator, in one machine, locally, today. That is a materially stronger offer than either a standard 14T or a bare cab kit, and it should be priced accordingly.

### The 14T — who to sell it to

**The GPS buyers** — anyone digging to a design:

- Civil contractors: drainage and pipe grades, detention basins, road subgrade, batters
- Commercial builders: building pads cut to design, first time
- Subdivision developers and their earthworks subbies
- Solar farm contractors: pad and pile accuracy across large areas
- Sports field, turf and arena construction: laser-flat surfaces
- **Tier-2 and tier-3 contractors who don't own machine control** — the biggest pool by far
- Tailings and water infrastructure work (Yanta already plays here)

**The rubber pad buyers** — anyone working on or beside a finished surface:

- Councils: footpaths, kerb and channel, car parks, park works
- Service stations, shopping centres, schools, hospitals, aged care, childcare
- Industrial estates and factories with concrete hardstand
- Plumbers and drainers doing repairs through existing driveways
- Occupied residential and commercial sites where damage means a claim

**Why the rubber pads argument closes deals:** steel tracks on a client's new concrete driveway means either laying protection mats or paying to resurface. You remove a cost and a risk they've been quietly budgeting for. That's a saving you can put a number on.

### The 7T telehandler — who to sell it to

- **Shed erectors and steel fabricators** — the single biggest telehandler market in CQ, and 7T handles portal frames and purlin bundles a 3T can't
- Industrial and mining maintenance, shutdown work, component change-outs
- Precast and panel handling
- Anywhere a crane is overkill, too slow to mobilise, or can't get access
- Solar farm construction

**The licensing point is a sales asset, not an obstacle.** With a jib and hook fitted and a rated capacity over 3 tonnes, the operator needs a **CN non-slewing mobile crane high-risk work licence** as a minimum (any slewing crane class also counts). Most customers won't have one. So:

> "It's a 7 tonne — over 3 tonne with a jib means it needs a CN ticket. We bring an operator who's got one, so you don't have to find one."

You've turned a compliance hurdle into a reason to buy wet hire at a higher rate. It's also why this machine is scarce in hire fleets — most stock sub-3T units precisely so anyone can run them.

## Pricing — what changes

Two things.

**1. The 14T is no longer priced as a 14T.** Machine control commands a premium in every market where it's offered, and the value story supports it: if it halves the time on grade work and cuts rework and over-excavation, the client's total job cost falls even at a higher hourly rate. That's the argument to make — not "our machine costs more", but *"you'll be off site sooner and you won't buy fill back"*.

**Don't let me invent the number.** Establish it properly this week:

- [ ] Ring **SITECH Solutions** and ask what a Trimble Earthworks cab kit rents for per week. That's the value of the technology alone, and it's your pricing floor above a standard 14T.
- [ ] Ring two competitors and ask what a GPS-equipped 20T goes for, wet and dry, in CQ.
- [ ] Set the 14T rate at standard-14T **plus** something approaching that kit rental value. You're supplying the kit, the machine, the calibration and the know-how.

**2. The 7T telehandler prices as wet hire by default.** The CN licence requirement means dry hire has a much smaller addressable market. Lead with wet, quote dry only when they confirm a ticketed operator.

**Keep the "no published price" position** — it's still right, and now you have a much better answer to "why not?": *"Because a GPS machine on a job with a design file is a different number to a bare machine pushing dirt, and I'd rather quote you properly than post a rate that's wrong for you."*

## Five things to nail down before you quote

These are execution-critical and I can't answer them from here.

1. **What's your GNSS correction source?** Trimble Earthworks needs RTK corrections to work — either your own base station or a network subscription (NTRIP/VRS). **If you don't have one, GPS dry hire is not sellable**, because the customer will assume it's included and it won't work on site. Find out what you've got and whether it's current.
2. **Who loads the design files?** Someone has to get the client's design into the machine. If you can do that, it's a chargeable service and a genuine advantage. If you can't, you need a surveyor on call — and it becomes a limitation you must disclose up front.
3. **What attachments does the telehandler have?** Jib and hook, forks, bucket, man basket? This determines the licence class, the market, and the compliance paperwork. A man basket brings its own engineering and inspection requirements.
4. **Have you got a CN-ticketed operator available?** If the wet-hire pitch is "we bring the ticket", that has to be true and current.
5. **Is the 35T GPS-ready?** If it's a modern machine with the harness, moving the Trimble kit between machines — or fitting a second — may be the cheapest capability upgrade available to you.

## The revised one-line strategy

> **Stop selling excavator hire. Start selling precision and access.** You've got the only local machine that can dig to a design *and* drive across a client's concrete, plus a telehandler twice the size of what everyone else stocks. That's a premium-rate, low-competition position — and it's aimed at contractors, not landowners.

Rural dam and pad work still has a place: it fills gaps in the calendar and it's genuinely quick money on Facebook. Just don't lead with it, and don't build the pricing around it.
